## Overview

Notion gives you a single workspace to store knowledge, run projects, and coordinate work without adding extra infrastructure. For security and compliance, the key idea is simple: you control who can access content, how workspaces are governed, and how data moves through connected tools. That matters whether you are evaluating Notion for a small team or rolling it out across a larger organization.

Use this guide to understand the main building blocks: encryption and access controls, compliance posture, admin settings for large teams, and scalable governance. You will also see practical examples you can adapt when you set up your own workspace.

## Data encryption and access controls

Notion’s security model centers on protecting data and limiting access to the right people. In practice, that means you should design your workspace with least privilege in mind: give people access only to the pages, databases, and team spaces they need. For sensitive content, use dedicated spaces and keep sharing boundaries clear.

Encryption protects data while it moves and while it is stored. Access control then determines who can open, edit, share, or administer content. If you are new to Notion, think of encryption as the transport and storage layer, and permissions as the workspace layer. Both are required for a strong security posture.

### Permission design examples

<CodeGroup tabs="Recommended,Too broad">

```markdown
## Recommended access pattern

- Create a private team space for sensitive planning.
- Share project pages only with the people who need them.
- Review guest access regularly.
```

```markdown
## Too broad access pattern

- Put every team in one shared space.
- Share entire workspaces by default.
- Leave guest access unreviewed for long periods.
```

</CodeGroup>

### What to check before you scale

1. Who can create top-level spaces.
2. Which pages are public, private, or shared with guests.
3. Whether external access is limited to approved collaborators.
4. How often you review permissions for active projects and archived content.

<Expandable title="How to think about least privilege" default-open="false">

Least privilege means each person gets the minimum access needed to do the job. In Notion, that usually means organizing content by team space, limiting broad sharing, and keeping sensitive pages inside restricted areas. It also means reviewing access after role changes, project completion, or offboarding.

</Expandable>

## Compliance certifications and evidence

When you evaluate Notion for a regulated environment, you usually need two things: a clear list of certifications and a way to confirm the controls that matter to your organization. Use the compliance program as one part of the decision, and pair it with your own internal review of data handling, vendor risk, and admin procedures.

Compliance is not only about the certificate itself. It is also about how you document policies, monitor access, and prove that the workspace is governed consistently. If your team handles customer data, legal records, or internal financial information, treat compliance as an ongoing process rather than a one-time checkbox.

## Admin settings for large teams

As your workspace grows, admin settings become essential for keeping structure predictable. You need clear ownership for spaces, a repeatable way to manage users, and a process for reviewing external access. The goal is to reduce confusion without slowing people down.

For large teams, standardize how you create spaces, name projects, and assign owners. This makes it easier to audit content later and reduces the risk of orphaned pages or over-shared documents. It also helps new teammates understand where important work lives.

### Example admin workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Define workspace owners" icon="users">
    Assign people who are responsible for security, structure, and support questions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Set sharing rules" icon="lock">
    Decide who can share pages externally and which spaces require extra review.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review guest access" icon="check-circle">
    Audit external collaborators on a regular schedule and remove unused access.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Document escalation paths" icon="help-circle">
    Record who handles incidents, approval requests, and policy exceptions.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Admin settings checklist

- Use consistent workspace naming.
- Keep sensitive teams in restricted spaces.
- Review guest access on a regular cadence.
- Assign owners for core projects and knowledge hubs.
- Document who approves changes to sharing rules.

## Scalable workspace governance

Governance is what keeps Notion usable as more teams, pages, and connected tools appear. Without it, content sprawl makes search harder, permissions messier, and compliance reviews slower. With it, you create a workspace that stays understandable even as the organization grows.

A practical governance model starts with structure. Define which content belongs in knowledge bases, which content belongs in projects, and which pages should stay private. Then set conventions for ownership, archiving, and review cycles. That way, your workspace stays organized and your security controls stay visible.

### Governance patterns you can adapt

| Governance need | Practical approach in Notion |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership | Assign a named owner for each critical space or database. |
| Review cycles | Schedule recurring reviews for permissions and stale content. |
| External collaboration | Limit guests to specific pages or spaces. |
| Content lifecycle | Archive completed projects instead of leaving them active. |
| Audit readiness | Keep naming and structure consistent across teams. |

<ExpandableGroup>
  <Expandable title="How to reduce workspace sprawl" default-open="false">
    
    Start with a small set of top-level spaces, then add more only when the structure is genuinely needed. Encourage teams to reuse templates and naming conventions so people can find content quickly.

  </Expandable>
  <Expandable title="How to keep governance lightweight" default-open="false">
    
    Use a simple review schedule, assign clear owners, and document just enough policy to prevent confusion. You do not need heavy process everywhere; you need predictable rules in the places that matter most.

  </Expandable>
  <Expandable title="What to do after a team changes" default-open="false">
    
    Recheck access, confirm the new owner, and archive pages that are no longer active. This keeps the workspace clean and reduces the chance of outdated permissions.

  </Expandable>
</ExpandableGroup>

## Best practices

Start with a small, well-defined security model and expand it as your use case grows. Keep sensitive content separate, review external access often, and document who is responsible for key settings. If your organization needs a formal approval process, define it before broad rollout so teams understand how to request exceptions.

Use Notion’s flexibility to your advantage, but avoid letting every team invent its own structure. The strongest workspaces are consistent, easy to audit, and simple to maintain. When you treat governance as part of the workflow instead of an afterthought, you reduce risk and make collaboration faster.

1. Separate sensitive and public-facing work.
2. Assign clear owners for important spaces.
3. Review access after every major team change.
4. Archive stale projects and pages.
5. Keep your naming and sharing conventions consistent.

<Callout kind="tip">

If you are preparing a rollout, write down your access model, review cycle, and owner assignments before you invite more people into the workspace.

</Callout>
